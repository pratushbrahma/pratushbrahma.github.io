What I work on

Every project starts with an animal I can’t stop thinking about, then whatever it takes to answer the question, whether that’s a notebook in the field or code on a screen.

Many animals, one big question

The fish that won’t quit: African jewelfish

African jewelfish don’t belong in Florida, but they’re spreading anyway. I build computer models of the choices they make about when to breed, and how those choices help them take over new water. Crack the game plan, and we get better at stopping invasions.

Does colour vision help my spiders hunt?

My main study species was Stenaelurillus lesserti, a termite-hunting jumping spider from India with unusually rich, four-channel colour vision. I tested whether that colour vision helps it find prey. The surprising answer was no, which hints its colourful eyes may matter more for courtship than for catching dinner.

Colour vision and foraging, compared

With the Taylor lab, I also helped compare two other jumping spiders: Habronattus pyrrithrix, which sees reds and oranges, and Anasaitis canosus, which does not. Here colour vision paid off. The colour-seeing species caught more prey when colour made it stand out against the background.

Would a spider eat a bitter bug?

Another Taylor-lab project asked how several jumping spider species respond to bitter, unpalatable prey. The differences help explain why some lineages evolved sharper colour vision: if you really need to avoid toxic, brightly coloured prey, seeing colour well is worth it.

From one fish to a whole invasion

What does a single fish’s choice mean for the entire population? I run simulations that connect one animal’s decisions to whether an invasion booms or fizzles. Exactly the kind of work that helps people keep invasive species in check.

Lizard turf wars: rock agamas

On sun-baked rocks in India, male lizards square off over the best spots and the chance to mate. I followed them around, mapped who held which patch, and tried to work out the rules of their stand-offs (with Dr. Kavita Isvaran, IISc).

Building a frog family tree

Teeny narrow-mouthed frogs from India’s Western Ghats. I helped figure out which species are related to which, drawing their family tree from their bodies and their DNA (with Dr. Karthik Shanker, IISc).

Where I started: cancer in the lab

Before the fieldwork, there was the lab. I studied how tumours trick the body into growing blood vessels to feed themselves, using a gene called FRG1, with mice and chicken eggs. Different world, same itch to know how living things work (with Dr. Manjusha Dixit, NISER).
